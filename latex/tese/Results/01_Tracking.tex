\section{Trajectory Tracking}
\label{sec:res_tracking}

% What is tested in this section?
Before the two vehicles operate together, each controller is evaluated individually. The evaluation first measures the accuracy with which the controller tracks a trajectory that is feasible for its vehicle. It then assesses the behaviour of the controller when the trajectory demands more than the vehicle can deliver, and whether the vehicle remains within its limits.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\gls{uav} Controller}{UAV Controller}}
\label{subsec:res_tracking_uav}

% Which reference, and why this one?
The tracking accuracy of the \gls{uav} controller is evaluated on a closed trajectory around the \gls{usv}, which holds its position throughout the test. The trajectory consists of two fast arcs, along which the distance between the vehicles is constant, connected by two slow turns, along which this distance varies. The \gls{uav} is therefore evaluated at both high and low speed, under a tether force that varies along each lap, since it increases with the length of the suspended tether. Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_scenario} shows the trajectory, coloured by the speed of the reference. The tether is long enough to cover the whole trajectory, so its length does not constrain the motion of the \gls{uav}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Results/figures/01_tracking/crescent_top.pdf}
    \caption{Reference trajectory of the \gls{uav} around the \gls{usv}, seen from above and coloured by its speed.}
    \label{fig:res_uav_scenario}
\end{figure}

% What is the NMPC compared with?
As a baseline, the same trajectory is tracked by the position controller of PX4, the standard controller for trajectory tracking on multirotors. At each instant, this controller receives the current point of the trajectory and acts on the error between this point and the current position of the \gls{uav}. The \gls{nmpc} receives the trajectory over a one-second horizon and predicts the response of the \gls{uav} and of the tether. The comparison therefore quantifies the benefit of prediction.

% Is the comparison fair?
The trajectory lies well within the limits of both controllers. The tether imposes the tightest constraint, and the trajectory respects it, keeping the distance between the vehicles within reach and its rate of change below the speed of the winch, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_feasible}. Since neither controller reaches its limits, the difference between the two results reflects only the tracking performance of each.

% How closely do the two follow it?
Both controllers keep the \gls{uav} on the trajectory, and over a full lap the paths are nearly indistinguishable from the reference, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_track}. The difference between them is of the order of centimetres. Over three laps, the position error of the \gls{nmpc} is more than four times smaller than that of PX4, both on average and at its maximum, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:res_uav}.

% Why does the error of PX4 grow with the speed?
The error of PX4 is not uniform along the lap. It increases on each fast arc and decreases on the slow turns, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_error}, which suggests a time delay, since a constant delay produces an error proportional to the speed. Such a delay is expected from a controller that corrects the error only after it occurs, whereas the \gls{nmpc} predicts the response of the \gls{uav} and anticipates it.

\end{figure}

% Does the NMPC need a larger effort to track more closely?
The higher accuracy of the \gls{nmpc} does not result from a larger control effort. Both controllers command nearly the same tilt and thrust along the lap, since both must produce the accelerations that the trajectory requires, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_commands}.

% What does the UAV do with a reference it cannot follow?
The behaviour of the controller at its limits is evaluated on a second reference, which demands more than the \gls{uav} can deliver, while the \gls{usv} again holds its position. The reference consists of two fast arcs centred on the \gls{usv}, followed by a step up and a step down in height, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_limits_top}. The first arc, with a radius of $15\,\text{m}$ flown at $10.5\,\text{m/s}$, requires a tilt of about $37^\circ$, well beyond the limit of $28.6^\circ$. The second, with a radius of $25\,\text{m}$ flown at $11\,\text{m/s}$, requires a tilt within this limit, but exceeds the limit of $10\,\text{m/s}$ on the horizontal speed. The steps then raise the reference by $7\,\text{m}$ and lower it by $10\,\text{m}$ to the surface of the water, below the minimum height of $2\,\text{m}$. Each phase therefore saturates a different set of constraints, while the separation between the vehicles remains within the reach of the tether.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Results/figures/01_tracking/limits_top.pdf}
    \caption{Reference beyond the limits of the \gls{uav} and path flown, seen from above.}
    \label{fig:res_uav_limits_top}
\end{figure}

% What happens when the tilt or the horizontal speed cannot follow the reference?
On both arcs, the \gls{nmpc} keeps the \gls{uav} on the constraint that makes the reference unattainable and follows the reference as closely as this constraint allows. On the first arc, the tilt reaches its limit in about $0.6\,\text{s}$, with its rate at the limit of $45.8^\circ/\text{s}$, and remains there until the end of the arc, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_limits_horizontal}. The horizontal speed settles at about $10\,\text{m/s}$, above the $9\,\text{m/s}$ that this tilt alone would sustain on a radius of $15\,\text{m}$, since the tether pulls the \gls{uav} towards the centre of the arc and adds to the horizontal component of the thrust. On the second arc, the horizontal speed settles on its limit and varies by less than $0.15\,\text{m/s}$ until the end of the arc, while the tilt remains below its limit. In both cases the \gls{uav} falls behind the reference, by $5.9\,\text{m}$ at the end of the first arc and by $4.7\,\text{m}$ at the end of the second. Since the shortest route to a point ahead on an arc lies inside it, the \gls{uav} also moves towards the centre of each arc, by up to $2.5\,\text{m}$ on the second, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_limits_top}. It recovers both the lag and this offset during the hold that follows each arc.

% What limits the motion of the UAV on the steps in height?
On the steps in height, the motion is limited by the vertical speed rather than by the thrust, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_uav_limits_vertical}. At the start of each step, the thrust changes at its maximum rate of $100\,\text{N/s}$, after which the \gls{uav} climbs or descends at $3\,\text{m/s}$. Since this speed is reached and lost with moderate accelerations, the thrust remains between $9$ and $38\,\text{N}$, within its limits of $2.0$ and $40.0\,\text{N}$. On the step down, the \gls{uav} decelerates ahead of the minimum height and stops at $2.00\,\text{m}$, although the reference lies $2\,\text{m}$ lower. During each arc, the height deviates from the reference by up to $0.6\,\text{m}$, a deviation that the \gls{nmpc} accepts while the tilt or the horizontal speed is at its limit.

% Are the limits respected?
The limits on the inputs are hard constraints and are never exceeded. The rates of the commanded roll and pitch reach exactly $45.8^\circ/\text{s}$, and the thrust rate reaches $99.3\,\text{N/s}$. The limits on the states are soft constraints, and the tilt, the horizontal speed and the vertical speed exceed them by at most $0.3^\circ$, $0.17\,\text{m/s}$ and $0.03\,\text{m/s}$, respectively, during brief transients. The minimum height is never crossed. In the holds after the second arc, the tilt varies by about $\pm 1.5^\circ$ around the value that balances the horizontal pull of the tether, whose tension fluctuates between $4.4$ and $5.4\,\text{N}$.

% What does this show about the controller at its limits?
At each limit, the \gls{nmpc} therefore drives the \gls{uav} onto the constraint, holds it there without oscillation while the reference remains unattainable, and resumes accurate tracking once the reference becomes attainable again.
