\section{Tracking and Planning}
\label{sec:res_tracking}

% What is tested first, and why in two steps?
The controllers of Chapter~\ref{chapter:methodology} play two roles at once. They follow the reference they are given, and they turn it into a motion that respects the limits of both vehicles and of the tether, whatever that reference is. The two roles are tested separately. The tracking is first measured with a reference that both vehicles can follow, so that the error reflects only how closely they follow it. The planning is then tested with references that cannot be followed, which jump from one point to another or lead beyond the reach of the tether, so that the limits decide the motion.

\subsection{Nominal Tracking}
\label{subsec:res_tracking_nominal}

% Which reference, and why this one?
In the nominal scenario, the \gls{usv} sails a circle of $3\,\text{m}$ radius at $0.15\,\text{m/s}$, while the \gls{uav} flies a circle of $5\,\text{m}$ radius about the same centre at $1.5\,\text{m/s}$, tilted so that its height goes from $21$ to $29\,\text{m}$ along each lap, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_nominal_scenario}a. Since the two circles are flown at different angular speeds, the \gls{uav} passes alternately above the \gls{usv} and on the opposite side, and the separation between them varies between $20$ and $29\,\text{m}$. The length of hanging tether varies with it, and so does the force it applies to the \gls{uav}, between $3.9$ and $6.0\,\text{N}$, Figure~\ref{fig:res_nominal_scenario}b. The reference is therefore easy to follow, but the force acting on the \gls{uav} changes at every instant. The maximum separation was set to $35\,\text{m}$ for this scenario, so that the constraint of the tether is never active.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Results/figures/01_tracking/nominal_scenario.pdf}
    \caption[Nominal tracking scenario.]{Nominal tracking scenario: (a) one lap of the \gls{uav} and the path of the \gls{usv}, with the tether drawn as straight lines at four instants, and (b) separation between the vehicles and tension of the tether at the \gls{uav}.}
    \label{fig:res_nominal_scenario}
\end{figure}

% What is the NMPC compared with?
To give these results a reference point, the same circle was also flown by the position controller of PX4, which is the usual way of flying a multirotor along a trajectory. It receives the position, the velocity and the acceleration of the reference at every instant, and follows them through its cascade of position, velocity and attitude loops, with the default gains of the x500. It is not aware of the tether, but it compensates any constant force through the integral action of its velocity loop, much as the disturbance observer does for the \gls{nmpc}. In both cases, the \gls{usv} is driven by its own \gls{nmpc}.

% How closely do the two follow it?
The tracking error was measured from the ground truth of the simulator, against the reference at the same instant, over $68\,\text{s}$, starting $15\,\text{s}$ after the start of the circle. Table~\ref{tab:res_nominal} and Figure~\ref{fig:res_nominal_error} show that the \gls{nmpc} follows the circle with an error of $4.0\,\text{cm}$, less than half of that of PX4. Neither controller keeps an offset in height, and the force of the tether is compensated by both. The difference lies in the timing. PX4 reacts to the error once it has appeared, and follows the reference about $45\,\text{ms}$ behind it, while the \gls{nmpc} anticipates its motion over the horizon. Most of the remaining error of the \gls{nmpc} is along the path, slightly ahead of the reference, by about $20\,\text{ms}$.

% And the USV, and the time taken by the solvers?
The \gls{usv} follows its slower circle with an error of $0.6\,\text{cm}$ and a heading error of $0.2^\circ$. Both controllers solve their problem well within their sampling period. The \gls{nmpc} of the \gls{uav} takes $4.8\,\text{ms}$ on average and at most $8.2\,\text{ms}$ of its $20\,\text{ms}$, and that of the \gls{usv} $1.9\,\text{ms}$ on average and at most $6.4\,\text{ms}$ of its $100\,\text{ms}$.

\subsection{Tether Constraint}
\label{subsec:res_tracking_tether}

% How is the planning tested?
To test the planning, both vehicles were given step references, single points held fixed over the whole horizon, that jump from one point to the next, with the maximum separation back at $15\,\text{m}$. The \gls{uav} starts close to the \gls{usv}, $8\,\text{m}$ high. It is first sent $10\,\text{m}$ sideways at the same height (waypoint~1 in Figure~\ref{fig:res_steps_tether}a), and then $12\,\text{m}$ higher (2), to a point $22\,\text{m}$ from the winch, beyond the reach of the tether. The \gls{usv} is then sent $18\,\text{m}$ away, against its heading (A), and finally both return to the start (3, B). Each reference only says where the vehicle should be, and the controllers alone decide how to get there.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Results/figures/01_tracking/steps_tether.pdf}
    \caption[Step references: paths and tether.]{Step references: (a) paths of both vehicles and their waypoints, with the height of those of the \gls{uav}, and (b) separation $d$ and its rate $\dot d$, with their limits. The vertical lines mark the steps of the \gls{uav} (solid) and of the \gls{usv} (dotted).}
    \label{fig:res_steps_tether}
\end{figure}

% What does the UAV do with a reference it cannot reach?
When the reference leads beyond the reach of the tether, the \gls{uav} stops short of it. Before it enters the cost, the reference is brought back within $d_{max}$ of the \gls{usv}, along the line towards it, so that the controller is drawn to the nearest point it can reach instead of pressing against the constraint. The \gls{uav} settles there within $2\,\text{s}$, at $14.0\,\text{m}$ from the winch, Figure~\ref{fig:res_steps_tether}b, slightly inside the limit, since the reference is brought within $d_{max}$ of the \gls{usv} at the water line, below the winch. When the \gls{usv} then sails away, the \gls{uav} follows it, still towards its unreachable reference, and the separation stays between $14.0$ and $14.4\,\text{m}$ until both return. It is the \gls{uav} that gives way. The \gls{usv} keeps its own reference, since the trajectory predicted by the \gls{uav} already respects the separation, and its own tether constraint never becomes active.

% And the rate of pay-out?
The pay-out rate is the constraint that shapes the first step. Moving $10\,\text{m}$ sideways at the same height would separate the vehicles faster than the winch can pay out the tether, and the \gls{uav} drops by $2\,\text{m}$ on the way, which slows the growth of the separation, and climbs back once it is there. The rate exceeds its limit of $2\,\text{m/s}$ only at the start of this step, reaching $2.5\,\text{m/s}$ for less than a second, while the \gls{uav} is accelerated before the prediction can slow it. The \gls{uav} reaches its waypoint in about $5\,\text{s}$.

\subsection{Actuator Limits}
\label{subsec:res_tracking_limits}

% Do the vehicles stay within their limits?
The same steps bring the actuators of both vehicles to their limits, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_steps_actuators}. At each step of the \gls{uav}, its thrust reaches its maximum of $39.96\,\text{N}$ and then drops to $5\,\text{N}$ to brake, while its pitch reaches the limit of $28.6^\circ$. The rates of the commands reach their limits without exceeding them, and the speed of the \gls{uav} remains below $5.7\,\text{m/s}$. When the \gls{usv} is sent backwards, its thrusters reach their maximum reverse thrust of $380\,\text{N}$ and their maximum angle of $30^\circ$ in opposite directions, turning the vessel in place before it sails away. No limit of the \gls{usv} is exceeded.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Results/figures/01_tracking/steps_actuators.pdf}
    \caption[Step references: actuators.]{Step references: tilt and thrust of the \gls{uav}, and thrust and azimuth angle of the two thrusters of the \gls{usv}, with their limits.}
    \label{fig:res_steps_actuators}
\end{figure}

% Where are the limits exceeded, and why?
Apart from the pay-out rate at the first step, the only limit exceeded is the pitch of the \gls{uav}, and only slightly. The commanded tilt never exceeds $28.6^\circ$, since the limit applies to the command as well as to the predicted attitude, and the real pitch exceeds it by at most $1.7^\circ$, for $0.6\,\text{s}$ over the whole run, as the attitude loop of the autopilot slightly overshoots its command. The steps take no longer than with a limit on the predicted attitude alone, which let the command reach $36^\circ$ and the real pitch $33^\circ$.

% What does this mean for the missions?
These results show that the controllers do not need references that are feasible, or even continuous. A reference can be a single point, and the motion to it is planned within the limits of both vehicles and of the tether. The manoeuvres of the missions rely on this, and give simple references, leaving the motion to the controllers.
