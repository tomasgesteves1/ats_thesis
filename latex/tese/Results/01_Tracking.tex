\section{Tracking}
\label{sec:res_tracking}

% What is tested first, and why in two steps?
The controllers of Chapter~\ref{chapter:methodology} play two roles at once. They follow the reference they are given, and they turn it into a motion that respects the limits of both vehicles and of the tether, whatever that reference is. The tracking accuracy is first measured with a reference that both vehicles can follow, so that the error reflects how closely they follow it. The controllers are then given references that cannot be followed, which jump from one point to another or lead beyond the reach of the tether, so that the limits decide the motion.

\subsection{Tracking Accuracy}
\label{subsec:res_tracking_nominal}

% Which reference, and why this one?
To measure the tracking accuracy alone, the \gls{usv} holds its position while the \gls{uav} flies the crescent of Figure~\ref{fig:res_crescent_top} around it. On the two arcs the \gls{uav} is fast, up to $5.9\,\text{m/s}$, at a constant distance from the winch, and it slows down in the U-turns that join them, where the distance changes. The tension of the tether follows the distance, between $3.2$ and $5.3\,\text{N}$, Figure~\ref{fig:res_crescent_separation}. The maximum separation is $35\,\text{m}$ in this test.

\subsection{Tether Constraint}
\label{subsec:res_tracking_tether}

% How is the planning tested?
To test the planning, both vehicles were given step references, single points held fixed over the whole horizon, that jump from one point to the next, with the maximum separation back at $15\,\text{m}$. The \gls{uav} starts close to the \gls{usv}, $8\,\text{m}$ high. It is first sent $10\,\text{m}$ sideways at the same height (waypoint~1 in Figure~\ref{fig:res_steps_paths}), and then $12\,\text{m}$ higher (2), to a point $22\,\text{m}$ from the winch, beyond the reach of the tether. The \gls{usv} is then sent $18\,\text{m}$ away, against its heading (A), and finally both return to the start (3, B). Each reference only says where the vehicle should be, and the controllers alone decide how to get there.

% What does the UAV do with a reference it cannot reach?
When the reference leads beyond the reach of the tether, the \gls{uav} stops short of it. Before it enters the cost, the reference is brought back within $d_{max}$ of the \gls{usv}, along the line towards it, so that the controller is drawn to the nearest point it can reach instead of pressing against the constraint. The \gls{uav} settles there within $2\,\text{s}$, at $14.0\,\text{m}$ from the winch, Figure~\ref{fig:res_steps_separation}. This is slightly inside the limit, because the distance is taken from the point on the water below the winch, and not from the winch itself. When the \gls{usv} then sails away, the \gls{uav} follows it, still towards its unreachable reference, and the separation stays between $14.0$ and $14.4\,\text{m}$ until both return. It is the \gls{uav} that gives way. The \gls{usv} keeps its own reference, since the trajectory predicted by the \gls{uav} already respects the separation, and its own tether constraint never becomes active.

% And the rate of pay-out?
The pay-out rate is the constraint that shapes the first step. Moving $10\,\text{m}$ sideways at the same height would separate the vehicles faster than the winch can pay out the tether, and the \gls{uav} drops by $2\,\text{m}$ on the way, which slows the growth of the separation, and climbs back once it is there. The rate exceeds its limit of $2\,\text{m/s}$ only at the start of this step, reaching $2.5\,\text{m/s}$ for less than a second, as the \gls{uav} accelerates towards the step faster than its prediction expects. The \gls{uav} reaches its waypoint in about $5\,\text{s}$.

\subsection{Actuator Limits}
\label{subsec:res_tracking_limits}

% Do the vehicles stay within their limits?
The same steps bring the actuators of both vehicles to their limits, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_steps_actuators}. At each step of the \gls{uav}, its thrust reaches its maximum of $39.96\,\text{N}$ and then drops to $5\,\text{N}$ to brake, while its pitch reaches the limit of $28.6^\circ$. The rates of the commands reach their limits without exceeding them, and the speed of the \gls{uav} remains below $5.7\,\text{m/s}$. When the \gls{usv} is sent backwards, its thrusters reach their maximum reverse thrust of $380\,\text{N}$ and their maximum angle of $30^\circ$ in opposite directions, turning the vessel in place before it sails away. No limit of the \gls{usv} is exceeded.

% Where are the limits exceeded, and why?
Apart from the pay-out rate at the first step, the only limit exceeded is the pitch of the \gls{uav}, and only slightly. The commanded tilt never exceeds $28.6^\circ$, since the limit applies to the command as well as to the predicted attitude, and the real pitch exceeds it by at most $1.7^\circ$, for $0.6\,\text{s}$ over the whole run, as the attitude loop of the autopilot slightly overshoots its command. Limiting the command does not slow the steps down. With a limit on the predicted attitude alone, they take the same time, but the command reaches $36^\circ$ and the real pitch $33^\circ$.

% What does this mean for the missions?
These results show that the controllers do not need references that are feasible, or even continuous. A reference can be a single point, and the motion to it is planned within all these limits. The manoeuvres of the missions rely on this, and give simple references, leaving the motion to the controllers.
