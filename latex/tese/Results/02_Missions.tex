\section{Missions}
\label{sec:res_missions}

% What do the missions evaluate?
With the tracking of each controller and the landing on the moving deck established, the evaluation proceeds to the system as a whole. In the missions defined in Section~\ref{subsec:missions_coordination}, both controllers operate simultaneously, coupled by the tether, and each executes the sequence of manoeuvres assigned to its vehicle. Three missions are evaluated, each forming a complete operation, from the takeoff of the \gls{uav} to its landing on the deck.

\subsection{Inspection}
\label{subsec:res_inspection}

% What does the inspection ask of the two vehicles?
The inspection represents the survey of a structure standing in the water, here a tower $43\,\text{m}$ high. The \gls{uav} circles the tower at $13\,\text{m}$ from its centre, in three laps at heights of $12$, $27$ and $42\,\text{m}$, with its heading pointed at the tower, so that a camera on board covers it from bottom to top, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_inspection_laps}. To reach the top of the tower, the mission is executed with a tether of $45\,\text{m}$.

% How does the USV support the UAV around the tower?
The \gls{usv} supports the \gls{uav} from the water, following it around each lap at the same angle about the tower and $12\,\text{m}$ further out, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_inspection_paths}. The tether therefore runs outwards from the tower on every lap, without crossing it, and the separation between the vehicles depends only on the height of the lap, remaining within the reach of the tether even on the highest one.

% Does the UAV hold its laps under the longer tether?
With the longer tether, the force on the \gls{uav} grows with the height of the lap, and its tension more than doubles from the lowest to the highest one. The position error of the \gls{uav} nevertheless remains of about $2\,\text{cm}$ on all three laps, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_inspection_error}, so that the two vehicles survey the tower from bottom to top at a constant distance, which neither could achieve alone. After the last lap, the \gls{uav} returns to the \gls{usv} and lands on the centre of the pad.

\subsection{Patrol}
\label{subsec:res_patrol}

% What does the patrol represent?
The patrol represents the surveillance of an area, in which the \gls{uav} acts as a lookout for the \gls{usv}. The \gls{usv} sails a route while the \gls{uav} orbits it, $15\,\text{m}$ above the winch, looking down with a camera assumed to cover $120^\circ$. Near the route, a moving target patrols a forbidden zone and flees when either vehicle approaches it. Once the \gls{uav} detects the target, both vehicles pursue it for one minute. The mission is executed with a tether of $20\,\text{m}$. Figure~\ref{fig:res_patrol_shots} shows the system at three moments of the pursuit.

% Is the target kept in view?
The target is detected at the edge of the view of the camera, beyond the reach of the tether, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_patrol_paths}. The \gls{uav} flies towards it until the separation reaches $d_{max}$, and then remains at the limit of the tether while the \gls{usv} closes the distance. Once the target is within reach, the \gls{uav} keeps above it until the end of the pursuit, so that the target never leaves the view of the camera.

% Does the UAV respect the tether?
Throughout the mission, the \gls{uav} keeps within both limits of the tether, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_patrol_timeline}. Besides the separation at $d_{max}$, its rate of change reaches the speed of the winch, $\pm\dot d_{max}$, as the \gls{uav} climbs to its orbit, sets off after the target and descends to land, and the two exceed these soft limits by at most $0.1\,\%$ and $6\,\%$, respectively. The \gls{uav} then lands $1\,\text{cm}$ from the centre of the pad.

\subsection{Recovery}
\label{subsec:res_recovery}

% What situation does the recovery represent?
The recovery represents the rescue of a \gls{uav} that cannot follow the \gls{usv}, for instance against a wind it cannot overcome. The \gls{uav} is held at a point fixed in the world, while the \gls{usv} is sent along a route of $60\,\text{m}$, three times the reach of the tether, with $d_{max} = 20.5\,\text{m}$ in this mission. After a wait, the \gls{usv} abandons the route and returns below the \gls{uav}, which then lands.

% Does the USV stop at the reach of the tether?
The \gls{usv} sets off at its top speed and brakes as the separation predicted over its horizon approaches $d_{max}$, and then waits at the reach of the tether, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:res_recovery}. While braking, the separation briefly exceeds $d_{max}$ by less than $0.5\,\text{m}$, since the \gls{usv} sees the \gls{uav} only over the horizon of the \gls{uav}, which is shorter than the time the \gls{usv} needs to stop from its top speed.

% Is the UAV recovered?
The \gls{uav} holds its point throughout the wait. Once the wait is over, the \gls{usv} turns back and sails below the \gls{uav}, which lands $3\,\text{cm}$ from the centre of the pad. The mission thus shows that, when the \gls{uav} cannot follow, the \gls{usv} moves no further than the tether allows, and can return to rescue it.
