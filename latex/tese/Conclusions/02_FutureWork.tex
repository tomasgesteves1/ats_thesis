\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:concl_future}

% What separates this work from real operations at sea?
The system was developed and evaluated in simulation. Bringing it to real operations at sea is a long path, of which the main steps are outlined below, each building on the previous one. They indicate a possible direction, and a system ready for real use would also require a much larger volume of testing.

% What hardware is needed first?
The first step is the hardware of the tethered system itself. The tether must carry power and data to the \gls{uav}, the winch must regulate its slack from the length of tether paid out and the distance between the vehicles, and both vehicles must carry \gls{rtk} satellite positioning receivers. On this hardware, the models would be validated again, and their parameters identified wherever the nominal values do not hold, in particular the hydrodynamics of the \gls{usv}, the attitude response of the \gls{uav} under its real autopilot and the mass and stiffness of the real tether. A formal analysis of the stability of the two cooperating controllers would also support their use on the real vehicles.

% Where would the state come from?
The second step is the estimation of the state. On the real vehicles, the state must be estimated on board from noisy sensors, such as satellite positioning and \glspl{imu}, and the controllers must keep their performance with this estimate. The cooperation in particular depends on the position of the \gls{uav} relative to the \gls{usv}, which the \gls{rtk} receivers can provide with centimetre accuracy by using the \gls{usv} as a moving base, without a fixed station on land. For the landing, this could be complemented by visual markers on the pad, which keep the relative position available close to the deck.

% What must the system do when something fails?
The third step is safety. A real system must detect and handle failures, such as the loss of communication, of satellite positioning or of power through the tether, a jammed winch or a broken tether, with defined responses for each, from holding position to an emergency landing. Operations would also need to comply with the regulations for unmanned aircraft and vessels.

% How would the vehicles avoid obstacles?
The fourth step is the avoidance of obstacles, such as other vessels, structures and the coast, which the system must perceive with sensors such as radar, cameras or the automatic identification system of vessels. Since both controllers already plan the motion within the limits of the vehicles, obstacles could be added to them as further constraints, both on the vehicles and on points along the tether, so that the tether does not catch on them. The horizons of the controllers, of $2\,\text{s}$ for the \gls{usv} and $1\,\text{s}$ for the \gls{uav}, would however see an obstacle only shortly before reaching it, and would have to be made longer, at the cost of a larger problem to solve at every instant. Alternatively, a planner over a longer horizon could give references that already avoid the obstacles, leaving the controllers to follow them within the limits of the vehicles.

% How would the system be brought to sea?
The fifth step is a gradual validation in the field, first with the tethered \gls{uav} over a static platform on land, then on the \gls{usv} in sheltered water, and finally at sea, in progressively rougher seas and stronger winds. Along the way, the manoeuvres closest to the deck would be made more robust: the landing could predict the motion of the deck to touch down when the deck is moving down, and the takeoff could start the disturbance observer before the \gls{uav} leaves the deck or turn the \gls{usv} so that the \gls{uav} faces the wind.

% What would real missions need beyond this?
Real missions would also require more autonomy than the fixed sequences of manoeuvres of this work. A reactive mission layer could choose the next manoeuvre from the state of both vehicles, for instance returning to the search when the target is lost or changing the route when the sea is too rough to land, under the supervision of an operator.
