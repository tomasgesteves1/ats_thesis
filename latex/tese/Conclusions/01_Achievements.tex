\section{Achievements}
\label{sec:concl_summary}

% What was the goal, and what was built to reach it?
This work set out to control a \gls{usv} and a \gls{uav} connected by a tether, so that they carry out maritime missions autonomously as a single system, each within its own limits and both within the reach of the tether, also under waves and wind. To this end, it developed models of both vehicles and of the tether, a controller for each vehicle that anticipates the motion of the other, a simulation of the tethered system at sea, and the manoeuvres and missions that put the two vehicles to work together.

% How good are the models?
The models were derived from the physics of each vehicle and evaluated against the simulator with its nominal parameters, without fitting them to the validation data. Over the horizons of the controllers, the model of the \gls{wamv} predicts its motion with errors below $1\,\%$ of the range of each state, and that of the x500 with errors below $2.5\,\%$, which for its position is $11\,\text{cm}$ over the up to $8\,\text{m}$ it travels in one second. For the tether, a closed-form model of its force, which accounts for its weight through the deflection of the force from the chord, reduces the error of the force by more than $70\,\%$ relative to a taut tether or a hanging load, to $1.2\,\%$ of the weight of the \gls{uav}. Unlike the massless and taut tethers or the lumped disturbances common in the literature, the model thus describes the force of a slack tether at little computational cost, and a disturbance observer compensates what it leaves out.

% How do the two controllers work together?
Each vehicle is driven by its own \gls{nmpc}, which respects the limits of the vehicle and plans its motion over a horizon of $2\,\text{s}$ for the \gls{usv} and $1\,\text{s}$ for the \gls{uav}. The two controllers exchange the trajectories they predict, so that both keep the separation within the reach of the tether, and the \gls{uav} also keeps the rate at which it grows within the pay-out of the winch, taking into account where the other vehicle is going rather than where it is. Both solve their problem well within their sampling periods, in $3.5\,\text{ms}$ of the $20\,\text{ms}$ of the \gls{uav} and $1.8\,\text{ms}$ of the $100\,\text{ms}$ of the \gls{usv} on average, so the cooperation does not require a single, centralised problem.

% What do the results show about tracking and planning?
On a reference both vehicles can follow, the \gls{nmpc} of the \gls{uav} tracks a circle with a varying tether force with an error of $3.8\,\text{cm}$, less than half of that of the position controller of PX4 on the same circle, since it anticipates the motion instead of reacting to the error. More importantly, the controllers do not need feasible references. Given single points that jump from one place to another, or that lie beyond the reach of the tether, they plan a motion within the limits of both vehicles and of the tether, with the separation never beyond $14.5\,\text{m}$ of its $15\,\text{m}$, the commands at their limits without exceeding them, and the tilt of the \gls{uav} at most $1.7^\circ$ above its limit. The manoeuvres can therefore remain simple, and give where each vehicle should be rather than how to get there.

% And about the missions?
The three missions show the two vehicles working as one system, with the normal tether of $15\,\text{m}$. In the scout, the \gls{uav} keeps ahead of the bow of the turning \gls{usv} within $5\,\text{cm}$ on average. In the inspection, the \gls{uav} circles a whole tower that the tether could not reach from any fixed point, while the \gls{usv} follows it from the water, at a constant separation of $12.3\,\text{m}$. In the patrol, the \gls{uav} detects a fleeing target beyond the reach of the tether and chases it, kept within that reach by its controller while the \gls{usv} pursues the target. In all three, the \gls{uav} takes off from and lands back on the deck of the moving \gls{usv}, $3$ to $5\,\text{cm}$ from the centre of the pad.

% And under waves and wind?
The landing on the moving deck holds under waves and wind. With the deck rising and falling by up to $0.44\,\text{m}$, the \gls{uav} lands within $4\,\text{cm}$ of the centre of the pad, and only the speed of the contact grows with the waves, up to $0.39\,\text{m/s}$. With winds of up to $12\,\text{m/s}$, the disturbance observer keeps the \gls{uav} within $20\,\text{cm}$ of the pad before it descends, and it lands within $8\,\text{cm}$ of its centre, while without the observer the offset grows to $83\,\text{cm}$ and, at $12\,\text{m/s}$, the mission fails. The most exposed moment is the takeoff, when a strong wind can tip the \gls{uav} against the pad before it is clear of the deck, even though the controller recovers it once in the air.

% So, what is the answer to the problem?
Together, these results show that two cooperating \gls{nmpc} controllers, with models of both vehicles and of the tether, can operate a tethered \gls{usv} and \gls{uav} as a single system at sea, in simulation. The tether, instead of being only a constraint to be avoided, is used through the cooperation of the two vehicles to extend what each can do alone, as the inspection of the tower shows. The planning carried out by the controllers keeps the missions simple, and the disturbance observer makes the system robust to the wind that would otherwise prevent its operation.
