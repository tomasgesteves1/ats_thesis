\chapter{State of the Art and Related Work}
\label{chapter:state_of_the_art}

Operating two vehicles as a single system rests on three elements, a model of how each vehicle and the tether behave, a controller that drives each vehicle within its limits, and a coordination that lets the two act together. The literature on each element is reviewed in turn, first through the techniques it offers and then through the works on tethered and cooperative maritime vehicles that apply them.

\section{Modelling}
\label{sec:sota_modeling}

Understanding how a system evolves is a prerequisite for any control design. Modelling provides this understanding by offering a mathematical description of the system dynamics, capturing the dominant behaviours while deliberately neglecting secondary effects that are not essential for analysis or control purposes \cite{modeling_Silva}. The level of detail of such a model is inherently linked to the available physical insight, the amount and quality of experimental data, and the specific objectives for which the model is intended.

\subsection{White-Box, Grey-Box, and Black-Box Modelling}
\label{subsec:sota_modeling_types}
% How are models classified by the knowledge they rely on?
Modelling methodologies are classified into three main groups, according to how much they rely on physical knowledge and on data. White-box models are built from first principles, expressing the dynamics through analytical formulations derived from Newtonian mechanics, hydrodynamics or aerodynamics. They provide clear insight into stability, controllability and energy transfer, and simplifying assumptions, such as linearisation or the neglect of higher-order effects, are commonly introduced to keep them tractable.

% How do grey-box and black-box models differ from white-box ones?
Grey-box models lie between purely analytical and purely data-driven approaches. Their structure follows from physical insight, while the parameters that are difficult to determine analytically, such as hydrodynamic or aerodynamic coefficients, are estimated from experimental data, which preserves a clear physical interpretation while improving accuracy. Black-box models, in contrast, rely entirely on measured input--output data and identify the mathematical relationships that best fit them, as in linear parametric models, state-space representations obtained from data, and learning-based methods.

\subsection{Tether Dynamics Modelling}
\label{subsec:sota_tether_modeling}
% Why does the tether need a model of its own, and what is the simplest one?
A flexible tether introduces highly non-linear forces and kinematic constraints on the vehicles, and three families of models describe it with increasing detail. Quasistatic catenary models describe the steady-state profile of a heavy tether suspended between two points, and obtain its sag and tension from the equations of static equilibrium, assumed to hold at each instant~\cite{irvine1981cable}. They are computationally efficient, but neglect dynamic effects such as transverse wave propagation and drag, and are therefore accurate only while the tether moves slowly compared with these effects.

% Which models capture the dynamics of the tether?
Lumped-mass models discretise the tether into a chain of nodes connected by spring--damper segments. The segments model axial elasticity, damping and hydrodynamic drag, while the nodes carry the gravitational and buoyancy forces, so that this formulation, implemented in solvers such as MoorDyn~\cite{hall2015moordyn}, represents transient whipping, slackness and drag with high fidelity. Finite-segment and multi-body models instead treat the tether as a chain of rigid cylinders connected by spherical joints, which captures bending and torsional moments accurately but requires solving high-index differential-algebraic equations, at a higher computational cost.

\subsection{Related Work on Tethered Vehicle Modelling}
\label{subsec:sota_sysid_related}
In systems connected by a tether, the model of the overall system must account at least for the tether geometry to constrain the relative motion of the agents and avoid physically infeasible configurations that could lead to tether failure.

\citet{kouraniMarineLocomotionTethered2021} present a buoy--\gls{uav} system in which the tether is modelled explicitly as a geometric and dynamic constraint between the buoy and the aerial vehicle. The tether tension is computed from the coupled dynamics and incorporated as an external force acting along the tether direction in the \gls{uav} model. This formulation relies on simplifying assumptions, namely that the tether is inextensible, massless, free of hydrodynamic effects, and remains taut during coupled operation.

\citet{yuReducedComplexityActiveDisturbance2023} propose an alternative modelling perspective, in which tether-induced forces are not included explicitly in the system dynamics. Instead, their effect is lumped into an external disturbance acting on the \gls{uav}. This disturbance is then handled at the control level through active disturbance rejection, avoiding the need for explicit tether modelling while still compensating for its influence in real time.

\citet{novakCollaborativeObjectManipulation2024} model a cooperative \gls{uav}--\gls{usv} system for the manipulation of a floating object. Both vehicles are connected to the object through individual tethers, and the full system is described using simplified white-box models. The formulation assumes inextensible and massless tethers that remain permanently taut.

\citet{zhangCooperativeControlFramework2025} consider no tether, and describe both the \gls{uav} and the \gls{usv} with simplified white-box models. The \gls{usv} is represented by a planar marine vehicle model commonly used for path-following applications, while the \gls{uav} is modelled independently using a reduced-order dynamic model.

\section{Control Strategies for Autonomous Vehicles}
\label{sec:sota_control}

Control theory provides the mathematical framework for designing systems that regulate their behaviour through feedback, ensuring stability, accuracy, and robustness in the presence of uncertainty and disturbances.

\subsection{Classical and Modern Control}
\label{subsec:sota_classical_modern}
Classical control is based on the representation of systems through transfer functions in the Laplace or frequency domain, under the assumption of linear time-invariant dynamics. Within this framework, feedback laws are typically implemented through \gls{pid} controllers and frequency-domain compensators designed to shape the closed-loop response. 

Modern control theory generalises these principles to systems that are multivariable, time-varying, or subject to strong coupling between states and inputs. It is formulated in the \textit{state-space} domain, where the internal dynamics of a system are described by sets of first-order differential equations. This representation enables systematic analysis of controllability, observability, and stability. Within this framework, optimal control theory defines feedback laws that minimise a performance index, such as the linear quadratic regulator or robust control methods such as $H_{\infty}$ \cite{andersonOptimalControlLinear1990,zhouRobustOptimalControl1996}.

\subsection{Predictive and Optimisation-Based Control}
\label{subsec:sota_mpc}
Predictive and optimisation-based control methods determine control actions by solving an optimisation problem that predicts the system behaviour over a finite horizon. At each sampling instant, the controller minimises a cost function subject to system dynamics and operational constraints, typically penalising deviations from reference trajectories and excessive control effort. Only the first control action is applied, and the process repeats at the next time step, forming the receding-horizon principle that defines \gls{mpc} \cite{camachoModelPredictiveControl2007}. Since the constraints are imposed on the predicted trajectory, the controller also plans a motion within the limits of the system, even when the reference itself cannot be followed.

Extensions to non-linear systems led to the development of \gls{nmpc}, which replaces linear models with non-linear formulations and uses numerical optimisation techniques to compute control inputs in real time \cite{gruneNonlinearModelPredictive2011,rawlingsModelPredictiveControl2020}. Its main difficulty is the computation time, since a non-linear optimisation problem must be solved within each sampling interval, which is short on fast systems such as \glspl{uav}. This has motivated solvers generated for a single problem before operation~\cite{verschueren2022acados}, and schemes that perform a single iteration per sampling instant~\cite{diehl2005rti}.

\subsection{Related Work on Tethered Vehicle Control}
\label{subsec:sota_tether_control_related}
In tethered \gls{uav} systems, the tether introduces additional forces and coupling effects that influence control design. \citet{kouraniMarineLocomotionTethered2021} address this coupling through a non-linear backstepping controller derived directly from the white-box dynamics of the \gls{uav}--buoy system. \citet{yuReducedComplexityActiveDisturbance2023} adopt an alternative approach based on active disturbance rejection, where the tether influence is not explicitly modelled but treated as an external disturbance estimated in real time by a reduced-order observer.

\citet{novakCollaborativeObjectManipulation2024} control the cooperative manipulation with two tethered agents using a \gls{mpc} formulation based on simplified white-box models of the \gls{uav}, the \gls{usv}, and the floating object. \citet{liNMPCbasedUAVUSVCooperative2023} present a similar predictive strategy, in which an \gls{nmpc} drives the \gls{uav} to track and land on the moving \gls{usv}, relying on known dynamic models.

In the work of \citet{zhangCooperativeControlFramework2025}, the \gls{usv} employs a line-of-sight guidance law combined with \gls{pid} control for heading and speed regulation. The \gls{uav} motion is controlled using an \gls{nmpc} formulation, which generates reference accelerations to track the desired relative trajectory. No joint or centralised control law is employed, and each vehicle is controlled using its own dedicated controller.

\section{Cooperative Coordination and Systems}
\label{sec:sota_cooperation}

Cooperative coordination refers to the control and organisation of multiple agents working towards achieving goals through communication and joint decision-making.

\subsection{Centralised vs. Distributed Coordination}
\label{subsec:sota_coord_architectures}
% How can the decisions of a team be organised?
Depending on how information is exchanged and how the control authority is structured, cooperative architectures are broadly classified as centralised or distributed. In centralised coordination, a single decision node aggregates global information and computes the commands of all agents. Since it has access to the full state and constraints of the system, it facilitates global optimisation and keeps the mission-level objectives consistent, but it scales poorly with the size of the team and is vulnerable to communication delays and single-point failures.

% What does distributed coordination offer instead?
Distributed coordination removes the central planner, and each agent decides from locally available information and limited communication, exchanging its state or intent with the others to achieve group-level objectives. This provides scalability and robustness to communication losses and agent failures, at the cost of challenges related to synchronisation and network stability.

\subsection{Related Work on Maritime Cooperative Robotics}
\label{subsec:sota_coop_related}
% What do UAV--USV teams already do together?
Cooperative deployments of \glspl{uav} and \glspl{usv} combine the speed and altitude of aerial sensing with the endurance and payload capacity of the \gls{usv}, and two applications recur in the literature, the autonomous landing of the \gls{uav} on a moving platform, such as the deck of the \gls{usv}~\cite{serraLandingQuadrotorMoving2016,liNMPCbasedUAVUSVCooperative2023}, and cooperative path following~\cite{zhangCooperativeControlFramework2025}. The works differ mainly in how the two vehicles are coordinated.

% How is the coordination organised in each work?
In the work of \citet{liNMPCbasedUAVUSVCooperative2023}, cooperation is based on a tracking strategy in which the \gls{uav} is controlled to follow and land on a moving \gls{usv}. Only the \gls{uav} is actively controlled, while the \gls{usv} moves independently and provides its state as a reference. The interaction is therefore unidirectional, with the \gls{uav} adapting its motion to the \gls{usv}.

In contrast, \citet{novakCollaborativeObjectManipulation2024} propose a centralised \gls{mpc} approach in which the \gls{uav} and the \gls{usv} are controlled jointly. A single optimisation problem computes the control inputs for both vehicles, explicitly accounting for the coupling introduced by the tether. This approach focuses on cooperative object manipulation rather than on mission-level coordination between the vehicles.

In the work of \citet{zhangCooperativeControlFramework2025}, coordination is achieved through a leader--follower structure. The \gls{usv} acts as the leader and follows a predefined path, while the \gls{uav} adjusts its motion to maintain a desired relative configuration with respect to the \gls{usv}. Coordination is therefore based on reference sharing and relative motion constraints, without joint optimisation or centralised decision-making.
