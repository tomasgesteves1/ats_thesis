\section{Models Validation}
\label{sec:model_validation}

Before incorporating the vehicle and tether models into the real-time controllers developed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:methodology}, their accuracy must be verified against the high-fidelity simulation environment described in Section~\ref{sec:simulation_environment} to ensure they capture the dominant dynamics while remaining computationally tractable. Each model is evaluated using the nominal simulator parameters without parameter fitting, driven by recorded inputs from representative manoeuvres. Model fidelity is quantified by the \gls{rmse} in physical units and by the \gls{nrmse}, defined as the \gls{rmse} normalised by the signal range.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\gls{uav} Model}{UAV Model}}
\label{subsec:uav_validation}

\paragraph{What must the model capture, and why?}
The translational model of Section~\ref{sec:mod_uav}, with the closed-form tether force of Section~\ref{subsec:mod_uav_tether}, is the predictive model of the \gls{uav} \gls{nmpc} of Section~\ref{sec:ctrl_uav_mpc}. For the controller, what matters is that it predicts the position and the velocity of the vehicle over the horizon $N T_s = 1\,\text{s}$ from the attitude and the thrust that are commanded. This relies on three assumptions: the vehicle reaches the attitude that is commanded, the commanded thrust is the force that the rotors produce, and a point mass subject to thrust, gravity, and tether is a sufficient description of the motion.

\paragraph{Against what reference, and with which parameters, is it validated?}
The reference is the ground-truth state of the vehicle in the Gazebo simulation, which includes the full six-degree-of-freedom rigid body, the rotor dynamics and drag, and the PX4 inner loops, none of which the model represents. As for the \gls{usv}, the model is evaluated with the nominal parameters of the simulator and no parameter is fitted to the data, with $m = 2.06\,\text{kg}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The thrust is obtained from the normalised command as $T = mg\,u_T/u_{hover}$, as in the controller, and the tether force from \eqref{eq:uav_tether_force}, with $c_d(\varepsilon_0)=0.2065\,\text{N/m}$ and $\gamma_0=28^\circ$ as in Section~\ref{subsec:tether_model_validation}. The attitude is given to the model in two ways, as measured and as commanded, which separates the error of the translational model from the error of the attitude assumption. The metrics are the \gls{rmse} and the \gls{nrmse}.

\paragraph{What data is used, and what does it cover?}
The data is a flight of $157\,\text{s}$ in which the reference is fed directly to the position controller of PX4 in offboard mode, instead of to the \gls{nmpc}. As the purpose of the flight is to identify the model and not to evaluate the controller, this gives commands that are independent of the \gls{nmpc}, and the attitude and thrust generated by the PX4 controller are recorded. The reference was built to excite the motion in all directions. It has two inclined lines flown at $5$ and $8\,\text{m/s}$, perpendicular to each other and each climbing from $3$ to $10\,\text{m}$, followed by a circle of $5\,\text{m}$ radius during which the yaw oscillates by $\pm 69^\circ$ independently of the motion. The vehicle covers $188\,\text{m}$ horizontally at up to $8.0\,\text{m/s}$ and changes altitude between $2.8$ and $9.8\,\text{m}$ at up to $2.6\,\text{m/s}$, while the distance to the tether anchor varies from $4.2$ to $14.7\,\text{m}$. The commands, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:uav_actuator_inputs}, reach roll and pitch angles of $25.5^\circ$ and $22.6^\circ$, and a thrust between $18.3$ and $26.4\,\text{N}$ around the weight $mg = 20.2\,\text{N}$.

\paragraph{Do the assumptions of the model hold in the data?}
The first assumption is that the attitude is reached as commanded. Figure~\ref{fig:uav_attitude_validation} shows that it is reached with a delay: taking the setpoint as the attitude gives an \gls{rmse} of $2.27^\circ$ in roll, $2.23^\circ$ in pitch, and $5.88^\circ$ in yaw (\gls{nrmse} of $4.76\%$, $4.92\%$, and $4.20\%$), with largest deviations of about $20^\circ$ where the command changes abruptly at the transitions between segments of the reference. A first-order lag with a transport delay of about $22\,\text{ms}$ explains the residual, with time constants of $0.121\,\text{s}$ in roll and pitch and $0.311\,\text{s}$ in yaw, and reduces the \gls{nrmse} to $1.47\%$, $1.16\%$, and $0.86\%$. The second assumption is that the commanded thrust is the thrust produced. The thrust acceleration implied by the measured motion has a mean of $9.905\,\text{m/s}^2$, against $9.901\,\text{m/s}^2$ commanded, which gives a gain of $1.001$ between the two, with an \gls{rmse} of $0.211\,\text{m/s}^2$ (\gls{nrmse} of $5.22\%$). The mapping through the hover thrust is therefore adequate over the whole range of the thrust.

\end{figure}

\paragraph{How closely does the model reproduce the reference, in the way it is used?}
At the level of the acceleration, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:uav_acceleration_validation}, the model driven by the measured attitude reproduces the motion with an \gls{rmse} of $0.397$, $0.418$, and $0.200\,\text{m/s}^2$ along $x$, $y$, and $z$ (\gls{nrmse} of $3.10\%$, $3.34\%$, and $3.94\%$), respectively. Driven by the commanded attitude, the horizontal errors become $0.632$ and $0.691\,\text{m/s}^2$ (\gls{nrmse} of $4.93\%$ and $5.51\%$). In the use that matters for the controller, the open-loop prediction over the horizon of $1\,\text{s}$, from the measured position and velocity, Figure~\ref{fig:uav_prediction_error} and Table~\ref{tab:uav_validation_metrics} show that the position error at the end of the horizon is $0.148\,\text{m}$ in \gls{rmse} with the measured attitude and $0.203\,\text{m}$ with the commanded one, with $95$th percentiles of $0.32$ and $0.45\,\text{m}$ and velocity errors of $0.263$ and $0.348\,\text{m/s}$, for displacements of up to $8\,\text{m}$ along the trajectory (Figure~\ref{fig:uav_prediction_trajectory}).

\paragraph{What explains the remaining error, and is the model adequate for its purpose?}
Three effects account for it. The largest is the attitude delay, which explains the difference between the commanded and the measured attitude, an additional $0.055\,\text{m}$ of position error after $1\,\text{s}$. The error concentrates at the transitions between segments of the reference, where the acceleration changes abruptly and the position controller commands tilt steps of up to $20^\circ$ that the vehicle follows with the lag identified above, and it is much smaller in the cruise and in the circle. The second is the aerodynamic drag, which the model does not include: a linear drag $\ddot{\mathbf{p}}_{xy} = -k\,\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{xy}$ with $k = 0.100\,\text{s}^{-1}$ accounts for only $11\%$ of the horizontal residual here and reduces the error with the commanded attitude to $0.190\,\text{m}$. The third is the tether term, which behaves differently. The closed-form force follows the force logged by the coupling node, with a three-dimensional \gls{rmse} of $0.424\,\text{N}$ and a mean of $[-0.207,\,0.120,\,-1.385]^\top\,\text{N}$ against $[-0.134,\,0.086,\,-1.383]^\top\,\text{N}$, which is about $8\%$ of the weight. However, adding it to the model increases the position error to $0.455\,\text{m}$, because only a small fraction of the logged force appears in the motion of the vehicle: a joint fit of the horizontal residual to the logged force and to the drag gives a gain of $0.11$ for the force. The validation therefore supports the model without the tether term, and the effect of the tether on the vehicle in the simulator is evaluated separately in Section~\ref{subsec:tether_model_validation}. For the controller, the point-mass model with the nominal thrust mapping predicts the position within $0.15\,\text{m}$ with the measured attitude and $0.20\,\text{m}$ with the commanded one, and below $0.45\,\text{m}$ in $95\%$ of the windows, over its horizon, which the feedback of the measured state corrects at every sampling instant, and it is adequate without further identification within the envelope of the recording, up to $8\,\text{m/s}$ and attitudes of $26^\circ$.

\subsection{Tether Model}
\label{subsec:tether_model_validation}

The control architecture developed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:methodology} requires an estimate of the three-dimensional force $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{tether}}^{\mathcal{I}}$ exerted by the tether on the \gls{uav} to compensate for tether coupling and enforce tension constraints. In simulation, the tether is represented by a high-fidelity lumped-mass model in MoorDyn, which captures multi-node elasticity, bending stiffness, and hydrodynamic drag. However, evaluating such a high-degree-of-freedom representation or solving the non-linear catenary boundary value problem numerically at every control cycle or across the prediction horizon of an \gls{nmpc} is computationally prohibitive. A closed-form, control-oriented model is therefore essential to provide instantaneous force predictions with negligible computational overhead.

The first-principles formulation derived in Section~\ref{subsec:mod_tether_force_model} computes the tether force directly from its nominal physical specifications under steady winch regulation. With the winch maintaining the slack ratio around $\varepsilon_0 = 0.05$, the linear mass density $\mu = 0.020\,\text{kg/m}$ and gravitational acceleration $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ yield a nominal tension-distance coefficient of
\begin{equation}
    c_d(\varepsilon_0) = \frac{\mu\,g}{1 - \varepsilon_0} = \frac{0.020 \times 9.81}{1 - 0.05} \approx 0.2065\,\text{N/m},
    \label{eq:tether_val_cd}
\end{equation}
giving the scalar tension estimate $T_{\mathrm{theo}}(d) = c_d(\varepsilon_0)\,d$, where $d = \|\mathbf{p}_v - \mathbf{p}\|$ is the Euclidean distance between the \gls{usv} and the \gls{uav}. In terms of the unstretched tether length $L$, this corresponds to $T_{\mathrm{theo}}(L) = \mu\,g\,L = 0.1962\,L$. The three-dimensional direction $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$ is constructed by tilting the line-of-sight chord $\hat{\mathbf{d}}_0$ towards the vertical by the nominal catenary elevation deflection angle $\gamma(\alpha) = \gamma_0\cos\alpha$, where $\gamma_0 = 28.0^\circ$ is the nominal deflection at $\varepsilon_0 = 0.05$ and $\alpha$ is the chord depression angle. To assess whether this nominal model adequately captures the coupling dynamics, its predictions are evaluated against telemetry from the 30-segment MoorDyn simulator recorded during a $60.5\,\text{s}$ flight trajectory with separation distances ranging from $4.6\,\text{m}$ to $14.0\,\text{m}$. Furthermore, the formulation is benchmarked against two widespread simplifications from the tethered \gls{uav} literature.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pure Vertical Ballast}, which assumes that the tether hangs directly downward as an equivalent vertical load with $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{pure\_z}} = [0,\,0,\,-T_{\mathrm{theo}}]^T$, neglecting horizontal coupling.
    \item \textbf{Pointing to \gls{usv} (Straight Chord)}, which assumes a taut tether between the attachment points with $\gamma = 0^\circ$, directing the full tension along the direct line of sight with $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{chord}} = T_{\mathrm{theo}}\,\hat{\mathbf{d}}_0$.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:model_fit_comparison} compares the predicted scalar tension magnitude $T_{\mathrm{theo}}(t)$ with the filtered MoorDyn telemetry over the trajectory. The theoretical linear relation with $c_d = 0.2065\,\text{N/m}$ follows the actual tension closely across the entire operating range, achieving an \gls{rmse} of $0.2031\,\text{N}$ and an \gls{nrmse} of $8.79\%$.

When resolved into Cartesian components in the inertial frame in Figure~\ref{fig:model_fit_3d}, the importance of accounting for the catenary deflection angle $\gamma_0 = 28.0^\circ$ becomes evident. The proposed formulation accurately tracks both the horizontal restoring force $F_x$ with an \gls{rmse} of $0.1457\,\text{N}$ (\gls{nrmse} of $5.19\%$), as well as the vertical downward pull $F_z$ with an \gls{rmse} of $0.2339\,\text{N}$ (\gls{nrmse} of $10.87\%$). In contrast, the pure vertical simplification fails to capture horizontal interaction forces with an \gls{nrmse} of $33.62\%$, while the straight chord approximation exhibits substantial errors in both axes ($21.85\%$ and $30.55\%$, respectively) because it ignores the natural sag of the tether.

The temporal evolution of the three-dimensional prediction error norm $\|\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{true}} - \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{model}}\|$, presented in Figure~\ref{fig:model_fit_residuals}, reveals the stability of each approach during dynamic manoeuvres. While the baseline simplifications experience large transient error surges exceeding $1.6\,\text{N}$ during vehicle reversals, the proposed catenary model maintains a low and bounded residual below $0.4\,\text{N}$ throughout the entire flight profile.

Table~\ref{tab:tether_model_benchmark} summarises the quantitative performance across the complete benchmark trajectory. The proposed first-principles model achieves an overall three-dimensional \gls{rmse} of $0.2757\,\text{N}$, reducing the spatial force error by more than $70\%$ compared to both the straight chord model with $0.8991\,\text{N}$ ($+226.1\%$ error) and the pure vertical model with $1.0332\,\text{N}$ ($+274.7\%$ error). These results confirm that the closed-form catenary approximation captures the dominant coupling forces with sufficient fidelity for real-time feedforward compensation, while avoiding the prohibitive computational cost of numerical catenary solvers or multi-segment models.
