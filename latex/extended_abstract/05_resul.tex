\subsection{Tracking}

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{nominal_error_column.pdf}
    \caption{Tracking error of the UAV on the tilted circle with the NMPC and with the position controller of PX4: norm of the position error and its vertical component.}
    \label{fig:nominal}
\end{figure}

The tracking was first measured on a reference that both vehicles can follow. The USV sails a circle of $3\,\text{m}$ radius at $0.15\,\text{m/s}$, while the UAV flies a circle of $5\,\text{m}$ about the same centre at $1.5\,\text{m/s}$, tilted so that its height goes from $21$ to $29\,\text{m}$. Since the two circles are flown at different angular speeds, the separation varies between $20$ and $29\,\text{m}$, and so does the force of the tether, between $3.9$ and $6.0\,\text{N}$. For comparison, the same circle was also flown by the position controller of PX4, which receives the position, velocity and acceleration of the reference, is not aware of the tether, and compensates constant forces through the integral action of its velocity loop.

Figure~\ref{fig:nominal} and Table~\ref{tab:nominal} show that the NMPC follows the circle with less than half the error of PX4, $4.0$ against $9.0\,\text{cm}$. Neither keeps an offset in height, and the difference lies in the timing: PX4 reacts to the error once it has appeared, and follows the reference about $45\,\text{ms}$ behind it, while the NMPC anticipates its motion over the horizon. The USV follows its circle within $0.6\,\text{cm}$, and the two NMPCs take on average $4.8$ and $1.9\,\text{ms}$, and at most $8.2$ and $6.4\,\text{ms}$, of their sampling periods of $20$ and $100\,\text{ms}$.

To test the planning, both vehicles were given step references, single points held fixed over the horizon, with $d_{max} = 15\,\text{m}$. The UAV is sent $10\,\text{m}$ sideways, then $12\,\text{m}$ up, to a point $22\,\text{m}$ from the winch, beyond the reach of the tether; the USV is then sent $18\,\text{m}$ away, and both finally return, Figure~\ref{fig:steps}.

When its reference lies beyond the reach of the tether, the UAV stops short of it, on the line towards it, at $14\,\text{m}$ from the winch, where the projection of its reference places it, and when the USV then sails away, it follows the USV, with the separation between $14.0$ and $14.4\,\text{m}$. In the first step, moving sideways at the same height would separate the vehicles faster than the winch pays out the tether, and the UAV drops by $2\,\text{m}$ on the way to slow the growth of the separation, whose rate exceeds its limit of $2\,\text{m/s}$ only briefly at the start of the step. The steps bring the actuators to their limits: the thrust of the UAV saturates at its maximum and its commanded tilt at $28.6^\circ$, while the thrusters of the USV reach their maximum reverse thrust and their maximum angle as it turns in place. The rates of all the commands reach their limits without exceeding them, and the real pitch of the UAV exceeds its limit by at most $1.7^\circ$, as the attitude loop of the autopilot slightly overshoots its command. The controllers therefore do not need feasible, or even continuous, references, which keeps the manoeuvres simple.

\subsection{Missions}

The three missions were flown with $d_{max} = 15\,\text{m}$, and are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:missions}. In the scout, the UAV keeps $8\,\text{m}$ ahead of the bow of the USV as it sails a route with two turns of $90^\circ$, with a mean error of $5\,\text{cm}$, largest at the turns, where the point ahead of the bow swings around the USV. In the patrol, Figure~\ref{fig:patrol}, the UAV orbits the USV until it detects a target fleeing at $1.5\,\text{m/s}$, $19\,\text{m}$ away from it and $23\,\text{m}$ from the USV, beyond the reach of the tether. Its reference above the target is brought within reach along the line from the USV, which also lowers it, so that the UAV drops from $10$ to $5\,\text{m}$ as it flies towards the target, and is above it $5\,\text{s}$ after the detection, with the separation at $14.7\,\text{m}$. As the USV closes in, at up to $2.2\,\text{m/s}$, the reference comes back within reach and the UAV climbs to its height again, and the USV catches up with the target $21\,\text{s}$ after the detection.

The inspection, Figure~\ref{fig:inspection}, shows best how the two vehicles work as one system. The UAV circles a tower of $12$ by $13\,\text{m}$ at a radius of $13\,\text{m}$, a circle beyond the reach of the tether from any fixed point. Its reference is a circle fixed in the world, regardless of the USV, while the USV supports it from the water, $6\,\text{m}$ further from the tower. The USV keeps within $3\,\text{cm}$ of its intended radius around the whole tower, the separation stays at $12.3\,\text{m}$, and the UAV follows its circle within $1\,\text{cm}$, so that the two vehicles together cover a circle that neither could cover alone. In the inspection and the patrol, the roll of the UAV reaches $32^\circ$ and $34^\circ$ for less than $0.5\,\text{s}$, above its limit of $28.6^\circ$, when its yaw lags behind its command, by up to $13^\circ$ in the patrol: the tilt is limited about axes turned by the commanded yaw, while the total tilt stays below the commanded one.

\subsection{Waves and Wind}

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{waves_landing_column.pdf}
    \caption{Landing with waves, from the start of the descent to the touchdown: height of the winch, height of the UAV above it, and horizontal offset from it.}
    \label{fig:waves}
\end{figure}

The disturbances were added during the landing on the deck of the USV, sailing a route at $0.75\,\text{m/s}$. With the gain of the wave field set to $0$, a calm sea, to $1$, the default, and to $2$, the winch rises and falls by $0$, $0.25$ and $0.44\,\text{m}$, Figure~\ref{fig:waves} and Table~\ref{tab:disturbances}. The landing takes the same $25$ to $27\,\text{s}$ in all sea states, since the reference is taken above the mean height of the winch, and the UAV touches down $0$, $2$ and $4\,\text{cm}$ from the centre of the pad. What the waves change is the contact: the final approach aims below the deck, so the speed of the contact is set by its motion, and grows from $0.15$ to $0.26$ and $0.39\,\text{m/s}$.

The wind was applied as a drag force on the UAV, with means of $0.9$, $2.4$ and $5.3\,\text{N}$ for $5$, $8$ and $12\,\text{m/s}$ and gusts of $\pm 30\,\%$, with and without the disturbance observer, Figure~\ref{fig:wind} and Table~\ref{tab:disturbances}. Without the observer, the UAV settles away from its reference, $18$, $44$ and $83\,\text{cm}$ from the centre of the pad before the descent, the landing waits for a centring that never happens, so that at $5$ and $8\,\text{m/s}$ it takes more than twice as long and ends $7\,\text{cm}$ off the centre, and at $12\,\text{m/s}$ the mission failed in both runs, once at the takeoff and once when the UAV, landing $0.8\,\text{m}$ off the centre, was blown off the edge of the pad. With the observer, whose estimate follows the force of the wind about $0.7\,\text{s}$ behind it, gusts included, the offset is $4$, $6$ and $19\,\text{cm}$ and the UAV lands within $8\,\text{cm}$ of the centre. The most exposed moment is then the takeoff, when a strong crosswind can tip the UAV against the pad while the winch still holds it, before the controller has any effect; the NMPC recovers it once it is released, after a drift of $5.5\,\text{m}$.
