\section{Results}
\label{sec:resul}

All results were obtained in simulation, with the default sea of the simulator, which moves the winch up and down by about $0.25\,\text{m}$, without wind unless stated otherwise, and with the state of both vehicles taken from the simulator.

\subsection{Tracking}

% How accurately does each controller follow a reference within its limits?
Each controller was first evaluated on a reference within the limits of its vehicle. The USV sails a figure-eight at the speed of the missions, with the UAV on its deck, and follows it within $5.6\,\text{cm}$. The UAV flies a closed trajectory around the USV, which holds its position, made of two fast arcs at constant distance joined by two slow turns, so that its speed and the force of the tether vary along each lap. As a baseline, the same trajectory was flown by the position controller of PX4, which acts on the error only once it has appeared. The NMPC tracks the trajectory with less than a quarter of the error of PX4, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:uav_accuracy}. The error of PX4 grows with the speed of the reference, which indicates a time delay that the prediction of the NMPC avoids, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:uav_accuracy}.

% What does each controller do when its reference demands more than its vehicle can deliver?
A second reference for each vehicle demands more than it can deliver. The UAV is given a tight arc that requires more tilt than allowed, a fast arc beyond its speed limit, and two steps in height, the second of which ends below its minimum height, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:uav_limits}. On each arc, the NMPC brings the UAV onto the limit that makes the reference unattainable and holds it there without oscillation, at the cost of a lag that it recovers once the reference stops. On the way down, it foresees the minimum height within its horizon and decelerates in advance, so that the UAV comes to rest at $2\,\text{m}$ although the reference lies on the water.

% And the USV?
The USV is given a fast leg, a sharp turn and two steps, astern and abeam, which drive both thrusters to their limits of thrust and angle, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:usv_limits}. In both vehicles, the limits of the inputs are never exceeded, and those of the states by less than $1\,\%$, so that each controller uses the full capability of its vehicle without driving it beyond its limits.

\subsection{Planning}

To test the planning, both vehicles were given step references, single points held fixed over the horizon, with $d_{max} = 15\,\text{m}$. The UAV is sent $10\,\text{m}$ sideways, then $12\,\text{m}$ up, to a point $22\,\text{m}$ from the winch, beyond the reach of the tether; the USV is then sent $18\,\text{m}$ away, and both finally return, while the separation stays within its limits, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:steps}.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{Results/figures/01_tracking/steps_separation.pdf}
    \caption{Separation of the vehicles and its rate in the step test.}
    \label{fig:steps}
\end{figure}

When its reference lies beyond the reach of the tether, the UAV stops short of it, on the line towards it, at $14\,\text{m}$ from the winch, where the projection of its reference places it, and when the USV then sails away, it follows the USV, with the separation between $14.0$ and $14.4\,\text{m}$. In the first step, moving sideways at the same height would separate the vehicles faster than the winch pays out the tether, and the UAV drops by $2\,\text{m}$ on the way to slow the growth of the separation, whose rate exceeds its limit of $2\,\text{m/s}$ only briefly at the start of the step. The steps bring the actuators to their limits: the thrust of the UAV saturates at its maximum and its commanded tilt at $28.6^\circ$, while the thrusters of the USV reach their maximum reverse thrust and their maximum angle as it turns in place. The rates of all the commands reach their limits without exceeding them, and the real pitch of the UAV exceeds its limit by at most $1.7^\circ$, as the attitude loop of the autopilot slightly overshoots its command. The controllers therefore do not need feasible, or even continuous, references, which keeps the manoeuvres simple.

\subsection{Missions}

The three missions were flown with $d_{max} = 15\,\text{m}$, and are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:missions}. In the scout, the UAV keeps $8\,\text{m}$ ahead of the bow of the USV as it sails a route with two turns of $90^\circ$, with a mean error of $5\,\text{cm}$, largest at the turns, where the point ahead of the bow swings around the USV. In the patrol, the UAV orbits the USV until it detects a target fleeing at $1.5\,\text{m/s}$, $19\,\text{m}$ away from it and $23\,\text{m}$ from the USV, beyond the reach of the tether. Its reference above the target is brought within reach along the line from the USV, which also lowers it, so that the UAV drops from $10$ to $5\,\text{m}$ as it flies towards the target, and is above it $5\,\text{s}$ after the detection, with the separation at $14.7\,\text{m}$. As the USV closes in, at up to $2.2\,\text{m/s}$, the reference comes back within reach and the UAV climbs to its height again, and the USV catches up with the target $21\,\text{s}$ after the detection.

The inspection shows best how the two vehicles work as one system, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:inspection}. The UAV circles a tower of $12$ by $13\,\text{m}$ at a radius of $13\,\text{m}$, a circle beyond the reach of the tether from any fixed point. Its reference is a circle fixed in the world, regardless of the USV, while the USV supports it from the water, $6\,\text{m}$ further from the tower. The USV keeps within $3\,\text{cm}$ of its intended radius around the whole tower, the separation stays at $12.3\,\text{m}$, and the UAV follows its circle within $1\,\text{cm}$, so that the two vehicles together cover a circle that neither could cover alone. In the inspection and the patrol, the roll of the UAV reaches $32^\circ$ and $34^\circ$ for less than $0.5\,\text{s}$, above its limit of $28.6^\circ$, when its yaw lags behind its command, by up to $13^\circ$ in the patrol: the tilt is limited about axes turned by the commanded yaw, while the total tilt stays below the commanded one.

\subsection{Waves and Wind}

The disturbances were added during the landing on the deck of the USV, sailing a route at $0.75\,\text{m/s}$. With the gain of the wave field set to $0$, a calm sea, to $1$, the default, and to $2$, the winch rises and falls by $0$, $0.25$ and $0.44\,\text{m}$, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:disturbances}. The landing takes the same $25$ to $27\,\text{s}$ in all sea states, since the reference is taken above the mean height of the winch, and the UAV touches down $0$, $2$ and $4\,\text{cm}$ from the centre of the pad. What the waves change is the contact: the final approach aims below the deck, so the speed of the contact is set by its motion, and grows from $0.15$ to $0.26$ and $0.39\,\text{m/s}$.

The wind was applied as a drag force on the UAV, with means of $0.9$, $2.4$ and $5.3\,\text{N}$ for $5$, $8$ and $12\,\text{m/s}$ and gusts of $\pm 30\,\%$, with and without the disturbance observer, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:wind}. Without the observer, the UAV settles away from its reference, $18$, $44$ and $83\,\text{cm}$ from the centre of the pad before the descent, the landing waits for a centring that never happens, so that at $5$ and $8\,\text{m/s}$ it takes more than twice as long and ends $7\,\text{cm}$ off the centre, and at $12\,\text{m/s}$ the mission failed in both runs, once at the takeoff and once when the UAV, landing $0.8\,\text{m}$ off the centre, was blown off the edge of the pad. With the observer, whose estimate follows the force of the wind about $0.7\,\text{s}$ behind it, gusts included, the offset is $4$, $6$ and $19\,\text{cm}$ and the UAV lands within $8\,\text{cm}$ of the centre. The most exposed moment is then the takeoff, when a strong crosswind can tip the UAV against the pad while the winch still holds it, before the controller has any effect; the NMPC recovers it once it is released, after a drift of $5.5\,\text{m}$.
